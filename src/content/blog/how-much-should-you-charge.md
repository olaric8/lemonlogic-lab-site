Here's the thing almost every small business owner in Nigeria wrestles with: *How much should I actually charge?*

It's not an invoice question. It's not a tax question. It's the question that sits in your head at night. You know you need to make money. You know your time is valuable. But the moment you quote a price, doubt creeps in: *Will they think I'm too expensive? Should I charge less to win the job? Am I underpricing myself?*

Most small business owners respond by either charging too little (and burning out) or charging inconsistently (and confusing clients). This guide gives you a framework to set prices that make sense — prices that cover your costs, reward your work, and feel defensible when you say them out loud.

## The three foundations of pricing

Pricing isn't magic. It's math plus psychology. The math is straightforward; the psychology is where most people get stuck.

### 1. Cost-plus pricing (the floor)

Start here: **What does it cost you to deliver this?**

For a service business, this means:
- Your time (how many hours does the job take?)
- Your overheads (rent, software, phone, transport)
- Materials or outsourced work (if any)

**Example:** You're a web designer quoting a 5-page website.
- Time investment: 40 hours
- Hourly cost to you (salary + overheads): ₦3,000/hour
- Base cost: 40 × ₦3,000 = **₦120,000**

That's your floor. You can't sustainably charge less than this without going broke.

But here's the trap: most small business owners stop here and quote ₦120,000, thinking they've done the math. They haven't — they've just calculated what keeps them afloat. There's no profit.

### 2. Value-based pricing (the real number)

Cost-plus tells you the minimum. Value-based pricing tells you what the client is actually willing to pay based on what the work is *worth* to them.

A 5-page website might cost you ₦120,000 to build. But what's it worth to a client?

- If they're a solopreneur using it to land clients, and it generates ₦500,000 in annual revenue, it's worth far more than ₦120,000.
- If it's their first online presence and they're testing the waters, it might be worth less to them — they can't risk big money yet.

**The framework:**
- **What problem does this solve for them?** (more clients, professionalism, automation)
- **How much is that problem costing them now?** (lost sales, wasted time, reputation damage)
- **How much will solving it make/save them?** (new revenue, reclaimed time, confidence)

A website that brings in ₦50,000/month is worth ₦600,000+/year to a business. Charging ₦150,000 for it is a bargain from their perspective.

### 3. Market pricing (the reality check)

What do others charge? Not to copy them, but to anchor yourself in reality.

For web design in Nigeria, market rates typically range from ₦80,000 – ₦300,000+ depending on scope and your experience. If you're new, you'll be at the lower end. As you build a portfolio, you move up.

Check what competitors charge. Ask peers. Look at job postings. Not to undercut them, but to understand where you sit in the market.

## The pricing formula (simplified)

**Price = Cost + Profit Margin**

If your cost is ₦120,000 and you want a 40% profit margin (standard for service businesses):

- Profit = ₦120,000 × 0.40 = ₦48,000
- Price = ₦120,000 + ₦48,000 = **₦168,000**

At 40% margin, you're covering yourself and building a buffer for slow months, investing in skills, and actually getting paid for the value you create.

For different business types, margins vary:
- **Service businesses** (consulting, design, writing): 40–60% margin is healthy
- **Trading/retail**: 25–40% margin depending on volume
- **Product-based businesses**: 50%+ margin (you need to cover inventory risk)

## The psychology: Why you undersell

Most small business owners underprice because of one of three fears:

**Fear #1: "They'll reject me if I'm too expensive."**
You might lose *some* clients. But you'll lose the *right* kind of clients — the ones who respect quality and commit. You'll keep the ones who see value and pay on time.

**Fear #2: "I'm new, so I don't deserve to charge much."**
You might be new at *running* a business, but you're not new at the *skill*. You have expertise. Price accordingly. You can always offer a small discount to your first few clients to build portfolio work, but then raise your rates.

**Fear #3: "If I charge what I'm worth, nobody will hire me."**
Test it. Quote your real number. You'll be surprised how many say yes. The ones who don't were never going to be good clients anyway — they would've haggled you down on price and complained about the work.

## How to actually quote

When a client asks for a quote, follow this:

**1. Clarify the scope.** What exactly are they asking for? (Vague scopes lead to underbidding.)

**2. Estimate your time and costs.** Be realistic. If something usually takes 30 hours, don't quote 20 hours to win the job.

**3. Calculate using your formula.** Don't guess. Use the numbers.

**4. Add a 10–20% buffer.** Things always take longer than estimated. Build in cushion.

**5. Quote as a package, not an hourly rate.** "₦168,000 for a 5-page website" is clearer and psychologically better than "₦2,100/hour." Clients hear the total, not the rate.

**6. Explain the value.** Don't just give a number. Say: "This website will help you land clients, and here's why it's worth ₦168,000 to your business."

## What about discounts?

Offer them strategically, not reflexively.

- **Bulk discounts**: "If you want 10 pages instead of 5, the per-page cost drops."
- **Loyalty discounts**: "Repeat clients get 10% off."
- **Cash discounts**: "Pay upfront and save 5%."

Don't discount because you're nervous. Discount because it's a deliberate strategy.

## On payment terms

Price and terms are linked. [If you've agreed on payment terms upfront](/blog/quote-vs-invoice-when-to-send-each) (50% upfront, 50% on delivery), your price can be firm. If you're unsure about payment, add a risk buffer to your price — you're working without security.

## The common mistakes

**Mistake #1: Matching a cheap competitor's price.**
They might go out of business in 6 months. Don't race to the bottom. Compete on quality, service, and speed — not price.

**Mistake #2: Quoting the same price for everything.**
A rush job, a complex project, a nightmare client — these deserve higher prices. Complexity commands premium rates.

**Mistake #3: Not raising prices.**
Inflation is real. Your costs go up. Your experience increases. [Raise prices annually](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time). Your existing clients understand. New clients pay the new rate.

**Mistake #4: Hiding your pricing.**
Transparency builds trust. The more you hide pricing, the more it feels like you have something to hide. Post it, talk about it, defend it.

## The bottom line

Pricing is hard because it forces you to claim that your work has value. But your work *does* have value — to you (you need to eat), and to your clients (they're hiring you to solve a problem).

Set your price using cost as the floor and value as the ceiling. Charge what your work is worth. If clients push back, they're either not a fit or they don't understand the value yet — in which case, your job is to explain it, not to discount.

Once you've set your price and confidently quoted it, the real work begins: delivering value that justifies it. That's where you build a reputation and attract clients who pay on time and refer others.
