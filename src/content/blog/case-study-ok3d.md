Logistics businesses have a specific invoicing problem that most guides don't address: the invoice isn't the only document in play. There's also the waybill — the record of what was moved, when, and for whom — and it has to line up with what gets billed.

OK3D runs delivery and logistics operations across multiple clients in Lagos, and this is a look at how invoicing and waybill tracking work together there, and what changed when the two stopped being handled as separate problems.

## The business

OK3D handles delivery and logistics work for a mix of clients — some one-off jobs, some recurring accounts that get billed regularly. Every delivery generates a waybill. Every waybill, eventually, needs to turn into an invoice.

That sounds simple until you're doing it dozens of times a week across different clients, some of whom get billed per delivery and others who get billed on a weekly or monthly cycle.

## The problem before

The core issue wasn't invoicing in isolation — it was the gap between the waybill and the invoice. A waybill would get written up when a delivery happened. Separately, at billing time, someone had to go back through those waybills and turn them into an invoice.

That handoff was where things went wrong:

**Waybills and invoices drifted out of sync.** A delivery would happen, the waybill would get filed, and by the time invoicing happened, small details — an extra stop, a changed rate, a partial delivery — were easy to miss or misremember.

**Recurring clients made it worse.** A client getting billed weekly might have five or six waybills to reconcile into one invoice. Doing that by hand, correctly, every single week, is exactly the kind of repetitive task where mistakes creep in.

**VAT got applied inconsistently across a growing invoice list.** With multiple line items per invoice — different jobs, different waybills — manually calculating VAT on a combined total left room for arithmetic error, especially under time pressure.

## What changed

Moving invoicing onto a proper tracked system — the same approach behind the [SME Invoice & Payment Tracker Kit](/invoice-kit) — closed the gap between waybill and invoice in a specific way: every job gets logged as a line item as soon as it happens, rather than being reconstructed from memory at billing time.

**Line items get entered per job, not per invoice cycle.** Instead of waiting until billing day to reconstruct a week's worth of deliveries, each waybill becomes a line item the moment the job is done. By the time an invoice needs to go out, the information is already there — nothing to remember, nothing to reconstruct.

**VAT calculates automatically across every line.** Whether an invoice has one job or fifteen, the 7.5% VAT calculation is consistent every time, the same way it should be [calculated on any invoice](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria) regardless of how many items are on it.

**Payment tracking works per client, not per invoice.** With recurring clients getting billed on a cycle, seeing which invoices are paid and which are still outstanding at a glance — rather than checking each one individually — cut down significantly on the admin overhead of following up.

## Why the waybill-invoice gap matters more in logistics

Most invoicing advice assumes a fairly simple pattern: do the work, send one invoice, get paid. Logistics businesses like OK3D don't work that way. The work is generated continuously — job by job, delivery by delivery — and invoicing has to catch up to it in batches.

That's a fundamentally different problem from a single freelancer sending one invoice per project, and it's exactly the kind of gap that generic invoicing advice or accounting software built for retail doesn't address well. The fix isn't a more complicated system — it's making sure each unit of work gets recorded once, correctly, at the moment it happens, so nothing has to be reconstructed later.

## The takeaway

If your business generates billable work continuously rather than as discrete one-off projects — deliveries, service calls, recurring jobs — the biggest invoicing risk isn't the invoice itself. It's the gap between when the work happens and when it gets billed. Close that gap, and the invoice becomes a formality instead of a reconstruction project.
