If you run a small business in Nigeria, VAT is the tax you touch most often — on the sales you make, on the invoices your suppliers send you, on almost every receipt. And yet it's the one most owners quietly fudge: adding 7.5% in their head, hoping the total is right, unsure whether they even need to charge it.

This guide fixes that. By the end you'll know exactly how to calculate VAT, how to show it correctly on an invoice, and what changed under the 2026 tax reforms — in plain language, with real Naira examples.

## First, a quick heads-up: FIRS is now the NRS

If you filed anything before 2026, you dealt with the **Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS)**. As of 1 January 2026, that agency has a new name: the **Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS)**. It's part of the biggest shake-up of Nigerian tax administration in a generation. Your existing Tax Identification Number (TIN) still works — but the letters, portals, and forms now say NRS. Throughout this guide, that's who we mean.

## What VAT actually is

Value Added Tax is a **consumption tax**. It's charged on most goods and services, and it's ultimately paid by the final customer — not by you. Your business is really just a **collection agent**: you add VAT to your prices, collect it from your customers, and pass it on to the NRS.

That framing matters. The VAT you charge was never your money to keep. Treating it as income is one of the fastest ways to land yourself in a hole when it's time to remit.

## The rate: 7.5%

Nigeria's VAT rate is **7.5%**. There was a lot of noise during the 2025 reforms about raising it — but that increase was dropped, and the rate stayed at 7.5% going into 2026. So the maths hasn't changed, even if the agency's name has.

## Do you even need to charge VAT?

This is where small business owners get anxious, and honestly, it's the one area where you should not take a blog's word as gospel.

For years, a business had to register for and charge VAT once its turnover crossed a set threshold in a 12-month period. The 2025 reforms widened the relief given to genuinely small businesses — the "small company" category (which also brings a 0% company income tax rate) is now defined around modest annual turnover and total fixed assets under ₦250 million, with professional-services firms specifically excluded.

But here's the honest part: **the exact VAT thresholds have been reported differently in different places** since the reform landed, and there have even been disputes about the final gazetted figures. So rather than give you a number that might not apply to your situation, here's the safe rule:

> **Confirm your specific VAT obligation directly with the NRS or a qualified accountant before you decide whether to charge it.** It takes one conversation and removes all the guesswork.

What follows applies the moment you *do* charge VAT — which, for most growing businesses that invoice other companies, you eventually will.

## How to calculate VAT: the two situations

There are only two calculations you ever need. Master these and you'll never fumble an invoice again.

### Situation 1: Adding VAT on top (VAT-exclusive)

This is the most common. You have a price, and you need to add 7.5% to it.

**Formula:** `VAT = Price × 7.5%` (or `Price × 0.075`)

**Example:** You're invoicing a client ₦150,000 for a website design.

- VAT = ₦150,000 × 0.075 = **₦11,250**
- Total the client pays = ₦150,000 + ₦11,250 = **₦161,250**

On the invoice, you show the ₦150,000, the ₦11,250 VAT as a separate line, and the ₦161,250 total. Never bury the VAT inside the price with no breakdown — a proper invoice always shows it separately.

### Situation 2: Pulling VAT out of a total (VAT-inclusive)

Sometimes you're handed a figure that *already includes* VAT — say a customer paid ₦107,500 flat and you need to know how much of that was VAT to remit.

You can't just take 7.5% of ₦107,500 — that's a classic mistake. Because the ₦107,500 represents 107.5% of the real price, you work backwards.

**Formula:** `VAT = Total × (7.5 ÷ 107.5)`

**Example:** A total of ₦107,500 that includes VAT.

- VAT = ₦107,500 × (7.5 ÷ 107.5) = **₦7,500**
- The actual price (before VAT) = ₦107,500 − ₦7,500 = **₦100,000**

### Putting it together on a real invoice

Most invoices have several lines. You total everything first, *then* apply VAT to the subtotal:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Website design | ₦150,000 |
| Logo design | ₦45,000 |
| **Subtotal** | **₦195,000** |
| VAT (7.5%) | ₦14,625 |
| **Total due** | **₦209,625** |

That's the entire calculation, start to finish. It's not complicated — but doing it by hand on every single invoice, and never slipping a digit, is exactly the kind of small repetitive task that eats an afternoon and eventually bites you.

## How to show VAT on your invoice properly

Getting the number right is half the job. A professional, compliant invoice should clearly show:

- Your business name, address, and **TIN**
- Your **VAT registration number**, if you're registered
- Each item and its price
- The **subtotal** before VAT
- The **VAT amount** as its own line, labelled "VAT (7.5%)"
- The **grand total** including VAT
- Invoice number and date

Showing VAT as a separate line isn't just tidy — it's what lets your business customers reclaim their input VAT, which makes you easier to do business with.

## Output VAT vs input VAT (and a 2026 change worth knowing)

- **Output VAT** is the VAT you charge your customers.
- **Input VAT** is the VAT you pay on your own business purchases.

When you remit to the NRS, you generally pay the difference: output VAT collected, minus allowable input VAT.

Here's the genuinely useful update from the 2026 reforms: **input VAT is now recoverable on services and on capital (fixed) assets — not just on goods**, as was the case before. That's a real cash-flow win. If you're VAT-registered, it's worth reviewing what you can now reclaim that you previously couldn't.

## Filing and the new e-invoicing rule

If you're VAT-registered, VAT is filed **monthly**, and the return for a given month is due by the **21st of the following month** — even in a month where you had no VAT to pay, in which case you file a "nil" return.

One important 2026 change: **e-invoicing is now mandatory for VAT-registered businesses.** The days of a Word-document invoice are numbered for registered businesses, so it's worth getting your invoicing onto a proper, consistent system now rather than scrambling later.

## Common VAT mistakes to avoid

- **Spending the VAT you collect.** It isn't yours. Set it aside the moment it lands.
- **Taking 7.5% of a VAT-inclusive total.** Use the ÷107.5 method instead.
- **Hiding VAT inside the price** with no breakdown — it looks unprofessional and blocks your customers from reclaiming input VAT.
- **Forgetting to file a nil return.** No sales still means a return is due if you're registered.
- **Assuming last year's rules still apply.** Under a name change and a full reform, check your position with the NRS.
