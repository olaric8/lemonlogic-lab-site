Ask a small business owner whether the business made money last month and you'll often hear "I think so." That "I think" is the problem. Money came in, money went out, some of it was the business's and some of it was theirs, and nobody wrote it down.

Bookkeeping sounds like something for accountants, but at the small-business level it's just keeping a clear record of money in, money out and money owed. Done monthly, it takes about half an hour and tells you things you can't see any other way.

## Why bookkeeping matters more than people think

- **You can price properly.** You can't set a profitable price if you don't know what the work really costs. (If that's your struggle, see [how much should you charge](/blog/how-much-should-you-charge).)
- **You'll know your VAT position.** If you charge VAT, you need to know what you collected and what you paid. [Getting the VAT right](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria) depends on clean records.
- **You can see cash problems early.** A profitable business can still run out of cash if clients pay late.
- **It makes loans, partners and tax conversations easier.** A banker, investor or accountant will ask for your numbers.
- **It protects you.** If there's ever a dispute with a client, supplier or tax authority, records are your evidence.

## Step one: separate business money from personal money

If you do only one thing, do this. Mixing the two makes every other record unreliable. Ideally, open a separate account for the business, even a basic one, and pay yourself a set amount rather than dipping into it whenever you need cash.

A registered business often finds it easier to open a business account, which is one practical reason to [consider CAC registration](/blog/cac-registration-do-you-need-to-register-your-business). But even without that, a separate personal-use account kept only for the business is far better than one shared with your household spending.

## The six things to track every month

You don't need dozens of categories. These six numbers cover most of what a small business needs.

**1. Money in (revenue).** Everything customers paid you this month, from every channel: transfers, cash, POS and mobile payments. Record the date, the customer and the amount.

**2. Money out (expenses).** Everything the business spent: materials, transport, data, rent, salaries, tools, bank charges. Group them into a handful of categories such as materials, transport, rent, wages and other, so you can see where the money goes.

**3. Money owed to you (receivables).** Invoices you've sent that haven't been paid yet. This is the number most owners underestimate. Knowing it tells you whether you have a profit problem or a [payment problem](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time).

**4. Money you owe (payables).** Bills you've received but not yet paid: suppliers, rent, staff. Cash in the account isn't all yours if bills are due next week.

**5. VAT collected and VAT paid.** If you charge VAT, track what you added to your invoices and what you paid on business purchases. Keep it separate from your sales figure, because it isn't your money to keep.

**6. Your cash balance.** What's actually in the account and in hand at month end. Compare it to what your records say it should be. If the two don't match, something is missing.

## A simple monthly routine (about 30 minutes)

1. **Collect everything.** Bank statements, receipts, invoice copies, cash notes.
2. **Record money in.** Match each payment to the invoice it settles.
3. **Record money out.** Enter each expense with a category and date.
4. **Update who owes you.** Mark paid invoices, list the unpaid ones and how overdue they are.
5. **Update what you owe.** List bills still to pay.
6. **Check the cash balance.** Does your record match the bank? If not, find the gap now, not in December.
7. **Look at the result.** Money in minus money out, plus what's owed each way. Is the business actually making money?

Do this on the same day each month and it becomes routine.

## Keep your receipts

Records are only as good as the evidence behind them. Take a photo of every receipt the day you get it, and save it in a folder named by month. Paper receipts fade and get lost, and a photo takes seconds. How long you must keep records can depend on your situation, so ask your accountant for the right period for your business.

## You can start with a spreadsheet

You don't need accounting software on day one. A well-organised spreadsheet does the job for most small businesses, and it costs nothing. If you're weighing your options, [free versus paid invoicing tools](/blog/free-vs-paid-invoicing-tools-nigerian-smes) covers when it's worth paying for more.

The invoicing side is where a lot of bookkeeping starts. When every invoice is consistent and shows its VAT clearly, [your invoices](/blog/invoice-template-checklist) become a clean record of what you're owed, and a payment tracker shows what's been settled.

## Common bookkeeping mistakes

- **Not recording small cash expenses.** They add up, and they're usually the ones that go missing.
- **Mixing personal and business money.** It corrupts every number.
- **Counting invoices as income before they're paid.** An unpaid invoice is money you're owed, not money you have.
- **Treating VAT as profit.** Collected VAT belongs to the tax authority.
- **Catching up once a year.** Memory fades and records go missing. Monthly beats annual.
- **Never comparing records to the bank.** Reconciling is how you catch errors early.

## When to bring in an accountant

A simple monthly routine handles your day-to-day records. An accountant is worth it for annual accounts, tax filings, payroll, anything involving VAT returns, and decisions such as registering a company or taking on a loan. Good records from you make their work faster and cheaper.
