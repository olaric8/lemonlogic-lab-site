You sent the invoice. You delivered the work. You've followed up twice. And then silence — or worse, a message saying "I'll pay you next month" followed by three months of next months.

Welcome to one of the most demoralizing parts of running a small business in Nigeria: the client who won't pay.

It's not just the money (though that hurts). It's the feeling that you've been disrespected. It's wondering if you should have demanded payment upfront. It's the time you spend chasing money that should have been spent on paying clients or growing the business.

Here's what to do when a client refuses to pay — and how to protect yourself before you're in this situation again.

## The first 48 hours: Stay calm and document everything

When a client goes silent or says "I can't pay right now," your first instinct might be to panic or get angry. Don't. Your second instinct might be to threaten. Don't do that either.

**What you actually do:**

1. **Send a professional written request.** Not a call, not a WhatsApp rant — a professional email or written message. State the facts: invoice date, amount, due date, and current date. Example: "Hi [Name], I notice Invoice #1234 for ₦50,000 (dated 15 August, due 30 August) remains unpaid as of today, 21 September. When can I expect payment?"

2. **Document their response.** Keep screenshots of every message, every excuse, every promise to pay. This matters later if you need to escalate.

3. **Don't mix emotion with business.** Phrases like "I'm struggling because you won't pay" or "This is disrespectful" feel true, but they close doors. Keep it factual: amounts, dates, expectations.

## Week two: Understand why they're not paying

There are usually three reasons a client won't pay:

**Reason 1: Cash flow problem (most common)**
They genuinely don't have the money. This is common in Nigeria — businesses operate on irregular revenue. They're not dodging; they're stuck. In this case, you might negotiate a payment plan: ₦25,000 this week, ₦25,000 next week. Written agreement. This isn't ideal, but it's realistic.

**Reason 2: Dispute about the work**
They say the work wasn't good enough, wasn't finished, or wasn't what they asked for. If this is legitimate, you have a real problem — you might need to either fix the work or negotiate a partial payment. If it's not legitimate (they're moving goalposts), document that too.

**Reason 3: Avoidance**
They're ghosting. They know they owe you, they know you know, and they're hoping you'll give up. This is where you need to get serious.

**How to find out:** Call them. Not to vent — to listen. Ask: "I'm following up on Invoice #1234. What's happening with payment?" Listen to the answer. Is it honest reluctance ("My account is frozen until next week") or avoidance ("I'll call you back")? The answer tells you what to do next.

## Week three and beyond: Escalate professionally

**If it's a cash flow problem:**
Offer a written payment plan. Get it in writing — email counts. Something like: "We agree payment of ₦50,000 will be made as follows: ₦25,000 by 1 October, ₦25,000 by 8 October." Make them agree in writing.

**If it's a work dispute:**
Offer to fix the issue or do a partial refund to close it. ₦40,000 instead of ₦50,000 is better than ₦0. Get written agreement that this settles the invoice.

**If it's avoidance:**
Now you escalate. In order:

1. **Send a formal letter.** Not angry, not threatening — professional. "This is a formal demand for payment of ₦50,000 on Invoice #1234, due within 7 days. Failure to pay may result in legal action." Send it registered (if they have a business address) or by email with read receipt.

2. **Involve a third party.** If they still don't pay, you have options:
   - A lawyer can send a demand letter (₦10,000–₦30,000 depending on the lawyer)
   - Small claims court in Nigeria can hear cases up to certain amounts (varies by state)
   - For larger amounts, you can file a case at the High Court, but costs escalate quickly

3. **Know when to stop.** If the invoice is ₦50,000 and you've spent ₦20,000 on lawyers and court fees, you've lost. At some point, the cost of chasing exceeds the amount owed. That point is different for everyone, but it's real.

## How to protect yourself before this happens again

You can't prevent all non-payment, but you can reduce it dramatically:

**Get payment upfront or in stages.** For big projects, ask for 50% upfront and 50% on delivery. For ongoing work, ask for payment weekly or bi-weekly instead of monthly. [This is covered in detail in our guide on getting paid on time](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time) — read it if you haven't.

**Use a payment system with receipts.** If you use the [SME Invoice & Payment Tracker Kit](https://selar.com/6w04585s89), every invoice is tracked, every payment logged, and you have proof of what was agreed. When you follow up on a late invoice, you have the exact record right there.

**Be selective about clients.** Some clients are always going to be late. Some will never pay. As you grow, you get to choose. A client who makes you chase payment for three months isn't worth the headache. Fire them professionally and move on.

**Write clear contracts or agreements.** Even a one-paragraph email saying "I'll deliver X by [date], you'll pay ₦Y by [date]" is better than a verbal agreement. Get it in writing.

**Know the debt laws in your state.** Nigeria's debt recovery laws vary by state. Some states have faster small-claims processes. Some have better mechanisms for enforcing judgments. Know what applies to you — a lawyer can tell you in one conversation.

## The emotional part: Knowing when to walk away

Here's the truth that doesn't get said enough: Some clients won't pay you no matter what you do. They'll make promises, string you along, and eventually vanish. Or they'll pay partially and ignore the rest.

**The question isn't "How do I make them pay?" It's "Is this client worth my energy?"**

If you've asked for payment, sent a professional reminder, offered solutions, and they're still ghosting — they've made their choice. You didn't fail. They did.

At that point, you write it off. Literally — if you keep books, it's an uncollectible debt. You move on. You tell yourself: "I learned not to work with this person again. That's worth ₦50,000."

And then you change your process so the next client can't do the same thing.

## The bottom line

Non-payment hurts. It feels personal. But it's a business problem with a business solution: documentation, clear communication, professional escalation, and knowing when to walk away.

The best protection is prevention — payment upfront, clear agreements, payment systems that track everything. But when prevention fails, you know the steps: stay professional, document everything, offer solutions, escalate if needed, and know when the cost of chasing exceeds the amount owed.

Most importantly: your time and energy are more valuable than any single late-paying client. Protect them fiercely.
