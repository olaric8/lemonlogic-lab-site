Most late-payment fights don't start when the invoice goes out. They start weeks earlier, at the moment someone said "just send me the details and we'll sort it out" and the work began with nothing written down.

When the client later says "I didn't agree to that price" or "I thought revisions were included," you have nothing to point to except your memory against theirs.

A written agreement doesn't have to be a thick legal document. For most small jobs, it's one page. Here's when you need one, and what it should say.

## Do you always need a contract?

Not always. A walk-in customer paying for a service on the spot doesn't need one. The receipt is the record.

You need something in writing when:

- **The work takes more than a few days.** The longer the job, the more room for the scope to drift.
- **You're paid after delivery.** Any time the client pays later, you're extending credit, and credit deserves paperwork.
- **The amount would hurt to lose.** If losing the payment would genuinely damage your month, put it in writing.
- **It's a new client.** You don't yet know how they behave when something goes wrong.
- **The scope is flexible.** Design, software, events, renovations and anything with "revisions" are where misunderstandings breed.

## Contract, quote or agreement: what's the difference?

For a small business, these overlap more than people think. A [quote](/blog/quote-vs-invoice-when-to-send-each) says what you'll do and what it costs. An agreement is a quote that the client has formally accepted, with the rules of the arrangement added.

In practice, the simplest approach is a quote with a short "terms" section at the bottom, which the client confirms in writing before you start.

## What your agreement should cover

Eight things. If you cover these, you're ahead of most small businesses.

**1. Who the parties are.** Full business names and contact details for both sides, not just first names.

**2. What you will deliver.** Be specific. "Website design" is vague. "A five-page website with a contact form and two rounds of revisions" is a scope.

**3. What's not included.** Say it out loud. Extra pages, extra revisions, rush delivery and travel costs are common sources of arguments.

**4. The price.** The total, and whether it includes VAT. If you're charging VAT, [show it separately](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria) from the start so the final invoice doesn't surprise anyone.

**5. The payment schedule.** When money moves: a deposit before you start, a balance on delivery, or stage payments for bigger jobs. State the dates or triggers, and the payment method.

**6. The timeline.** Start date, delivery date, and what happens if the client's delay (late feedback, missing materials) pushes things back.

**7. Late payment terms.** What happens if an invoice isn't paid by the due date. Setting this in advance makes [chasing payment](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time) a matter of following the agreement rather than an awkward personal request.

**8. Changes and cancellation.** How extra requests are handled and priced, and what happens to the deposit if either side pulls out.

## Get a deposit before you start

If there's one habit that prevents more pain than any document, it's this: never start significant work without a deposit.

A deposit does two things. It covers your early costs, and it filters out clients who aren't serious. Many small businesses ask for 30 to 50 percent upfront on larger jobs, with the balance due on delivery. Whatever split you choose, [price the job properly](/blog/how-much-should-you-charge) first so the deposit actually covers your real costs.

## Does a WhatsApp conversation count?

Often it counts as evidence, but it's a poor substitute for a clear agreement. Messages are scattered, ambiguous and easy to dispute.

If the deal happens on WhatsApp, finish with a summary message: "Just confirming: five pages, two revision rounds, NGN 150,000, 50% deposit before we start, balance on delivery. Please reply 'agreed' to confirm." A clear written reply to that is far better than a trail of loose chat.

## How it connects to your invoice

Your invoice should match your agreement exactly: same scope, same price, same terms. When it does, there's nothing to argue about. When it doesn't, the invoice becomes the first place a dispute shows up. Run every invoice through the [12-point invoice checklist](/blog/invoice-template-checklist) before sending, and if a client still refuses to pay, your agreement is what makes [the next steps](/blog/what-to-do-when-client-refuses-to-pay) possible.

## Keep it simple

You don't need a lawyer for every job. A clear one-page agreement, confirmed by the client in writing, solves most problems before they begin. For large contracts, long-term arrangements or anything involving significant sums, get a qualified lawyer to review it.
