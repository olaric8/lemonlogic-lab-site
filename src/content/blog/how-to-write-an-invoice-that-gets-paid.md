You did the work. You delivered. You sent the invoice. And then... nothing. A week passes. Two. You send a "just following up" message and feel slightly embarrassed doing it.

Every Nigerian business owner knows this dance. But here's what most don't realise: **a big part of getting paid on time is the invoice itself.** A vague, unprofessional, or confusing invoice gives a client every excuse to delay. A clear, professional one removes those excuses — it tells the client exactly what they owe, why, when, and how to pay, with no friction and no ambiguity.

This guide breaks down how to write an invoice that gets you paid — the elements that matter, the ones that quietly cost you money when they're missing, and the small habits that turn "chasing payments" into "getting paid on time."

## An invoice is a tool, not just a bill

Reframe it in your mind: an invoice's job is to make paying you the easiest possible thing for your client to do. Every unclear detail is a reason to set it aside "for later." Every missing piece is a follow-up message you'll have to send. So the goal isn't just to *record* what you're owed — it's to design a document that gets actioned fast.

With that in mind, here's the anatomy.

## The anatomy of an invoice that gets paid

**1. The word "Invoice" and a unique invoice number.** State clearly that it's an invoice — not a "note" or a quote. Give every invoice a unique number (INV-001, INV-002, and so on). This isn't bureaucracy: numbered invoices look organised, make follow-ups precise ("regarding invoice INV-014"), and keep your own records clean.

**2. Your business details — and your TIN.** Your business name, address, phone, email, and logo. Include your **Tax Identification Number (TIN)**. This does more than look official; a business that presents a proper, complete invoice signals that it's serious and established — which makes clients more comfortable paying promptly.

**3. The client's correct details.** Their business name (spelled correctly), address, and a contact. This matters for *their* records — a business client often can't process payment internally if the invoice doesn't match their books.

**4. Issue date and — critically — a due date.** This is the single most important element for getting paid on time. "Payment due on delivery" or an open-ended invoice invites indefinite delay. A specific due date ("Due: 25 September 2026") creates a clear deadline and a psychological anchor. No due date, no urgency.

**5. Clear line items.** Don't write "Services rendered — ₦200,000." Break it down: what you did, the quantity, the rate, and the amount for each line. Specificity builds trust and pre-empts the "what exactly am I paying for?" question that stalls payment.

**6. Subtotal, VAT, and a total that can't be missed.** Show the subtotal, then VAT at 7.5% as its own line (if you charge it — we covered exactly how to calculate it in [How to Calculate VAT in Nigeria](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria)), then the **grand total due**, in bold, large, unmissable. The client should see what they owe in one glance.

**7. Payment details — make it frictionless.** Your bank name, account number, and account name, clearly laid out. This sounds obvious, but a shocking number of invoices bury or omit it, forcing the client to ask — which delays payment by days. If you accept multiple methods (transfer, a payment link), list them. The easier you make it to pay, the faster you get paid.

**8. Payment terms.** Spell out the terms: "Payment due within 7 days of invoice date." If you charge a late fee, state it here. Clear terms set expectations and give you a firm, professional footing if you need to follow up.

**9. A brief, warm closing note.** A simple "Thank you for your business" costs nothing and keeps the relationship human. Payment is a relationship transaction, not just a financial one.

## Getting paid is also about habits, not just layout

A perfect invoice still needs good habits around it:

- **Agree terms before the work, not after.** The best time to set payment expectations is before you start — in your quote or agreement. An invoice should confirm what was already agreed, not surprise anyone.
- **Send it immediately.** The moment the work is done, send the invoice. Promptness signals professionalism, and it starts the payment clock ticking sooner. An invoice sent two weeks late tells the client that timing doesn't matter much to you.
- **Send a friendly reminder — twice.** A short, polite note a day or two *before* the due date ("just a heads-up, invoice INV-014 is due Friday") and again if it passes unpaid. Most late payments aren't refusals; they're simply forgotten. A reminder isn't rude — it's part of the job.
- **Keep a record of what's outstanding.** You can't chase what you can't see. A simple tracker of who owes what, and which invoices are paid, overdue, or pending, turns payment-chasing from stressful guesswork into a two-minute glance.

## One thing on the horizon: NRS e-invoicing

You may have heard about **e-invoicing** becoming mandatory in Nigeria. Here's the calm version of the truth, so you neither panic nor get caught out.

Under the 2025 tax reforms, the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS) — the renamed FIRS — is rolling out a mandatory electronic invoicing system (the Merchant Buyer Solution) in **phases, based on turnover**. Large businesses are already on it. But the rollout for **small and "emerging" businesses (turnover under ₦1 billion) isn't scheduled to go live until around mid-2027, with enforcement following into 2028.**

In other words: if you're a typical small business, **you are not required to use the NRS e-invoicing system yet.** But it is coming. The smartest move now isn't to scramble — it's to get your invoicing onto a **clean, consistent, professional format today**, so that when the requirement reaches your tier, you're adjusting a system you already run well rather than building one from scratch under a deadline. (Thresholds and dates are set by the NRS and can change — confirm your position with the NRS or your accountant.)

## Common mistakes that delay your payment

- **No due date** — the number-one cause of "I'll pay you later" becoming "never."
- **Vague line items** — "services rendered" invites questions; questions cause delays.
- **Missing or buried bank details** — every extra step you make the client take costs you days.
- **Inconsistent, ad-hoc invoices** — a different-looking invoice every time looks disorganised and erodes trust.
- **Not following up** — silence after sending isn't patience; it's leaving money on the table.
- **No record of what's owed** — if you're not tracking it, you're not collecting all of it.
