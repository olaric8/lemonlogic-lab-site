If you've been in business for any length of time, you've probably sent both — and you might have used them interchangeably. A lot of Nigerian business owners do. And it costs them.

A quote and an invoice look similar. They both have prices. They both go to clients. But they do **completely different jobs**, and using the wrong one at the wrong time creates confusion, leaves money on the table, and makes you look disorganised.

This is a short guide that clears it up: what each one is, when to send it, and what happens when you mix them up.

## The core difference: promise vs. demand

**A quote is a promise.** It says: "If you want this, it will cost this much." It's an offer. The client can accept it, reject it, or negotiate. Until they say yes, you've made no deal and done no work.

**An invoice is a demand.** It says: "You owe me this much for work I did." It's a record of a transaction that already happened, or is agreed to happen. An invoice is what triggers payment.

That one difference changes everything about when and how you use them.

## The quote: before the work

Send a quote **before** you start any project. It's your formal offer to a prospective client.

A good quote includes:
- **What you're offering** — the exact work, deliverable, or product
- **How much it costs** — break it down by line item so the client sees the value
- **The conditions** — payment terms, timeline, any assumptions (e.g., "includes 2 rounds of revisions, extras charged separately")
- **An expiry date** — "This quote is valid until 30 September 2026." This creates urgency and protects you if costs shift

The goal of a quote is to **get agreement before you commit time or money**. Until the client signs off or says "let's go," you're not obligated, and they're not.

A quote does two jobs:
1. **Protects you** — no surprises about scope or price later
2. **Helps the client decide** — a clear quote makes it easy to say yes or compare your offer against competitors

Once the client agrees ("Yes, go ahead with the ₦150,000 proposal"), you're ready to start.

## The invoice: after the work (or at agreed milestones)

Send an invoice **after** you've delivered the work — or, for longer projects, at agreed milestones or at the end of each month.

An invoice should reference the original quote (or at minimum, the agreement): "Per our proposal dated 15 September, invoice for web design services." This reminds the client what they agreed to and why they're paying.

A good invoice includes everything from the quote **plus**:
- **Invoice number** — unique ID for tracking
- **Issue date and due date** — the critical dates for payment
- **Your payment details** — bank account, transfer method
- **VAT** — if you charge it, [calculated at 7.5%](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria)
- **A record of what was delivered** — if the project took stages, spell them out

The goal of an invoice is to **collect payment**. It's a legal record of what's owed and when.

Here's how they fit together in a real project:

1. **Client asks for a proposal** (or you reach out and suggest a solution)
2. **You send a quote** with the scope, price, and conditions
3. **Client agrees** ("Yes, let's do it") — formally or just verbally
4. **You do the work**
5. **You send an invoice** with the final details and your payment terms
6. **Client pays by the due date**

That's the clean flow. But things get messy when people skip steps or blur them.

## What goes wrong when you mix them up

**Sending an invoice without a quote first:**
You do work, send an invoice, and the client says "I didn't agree to this price." Because you never showed them the terms upfront, you have no ground to stand on. They feel ambushed; you feel owed. Result: payment delay, negotiation, or dispute.

**Sending a quote and never following up with an invoice:**
The client agrees verbally, you do the work, and then... nothing. No formal invoice sent. They assume they'll get one, you assume the agreement was clear. Weeks pass. Payment dribbles in late or not at all. Without an invoice, you have no formal record of what's owed or when.

**Using the same document for both:**
Some businesses send a single "quote/invoice" hybrid — which looks unprofessional and confuses the client about whether it's a proposal or a demand. It's neither, cleanly.

## One more thing: interim invoices and retainers

For longer projects, you might send **interim invoices** at milestones (e.g., "50% due on project start, 50% on delivery"). Reference the original quote, but each interim invoice should be numbered and dated separately. This keeps cash flowing and protects you if a project stalls.

If a client pays a retainer upfront (common for consulting or ongoing services), send an invoice for the retainer even if no work has happened yet. This formalises the transaction and gives them a record. Then, as you deliver work, you either invoice against the retainer or invoice separately.
