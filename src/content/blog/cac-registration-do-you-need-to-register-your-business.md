Plenty of Nigerian small businesses run for years without any formal registration. They get customers, send invoices and make money. So is CAC registration really necessary, or is it paperwork you can keep postponing?

The honest answer is that it depends on who you sell to, how you get paid and what you stand to lose. Here's a practical way to think about it.

## What the CAC actually registers

The Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) is Nigeria's corporate registry. For most small businesses, the choice comes down to two structures.

**Business name (sole proprietorship or partnership).** The simplest option. It lets you trade under a name that isn't your personal name. It's usually the cheaper and faster route, but there is no legal separation between you and the business. If the business owes money, your personal assets can be exposed.

**Limited liability company (LTD).** A separate legal entity from its owners. It can sign contracts, own property and be sued in its own name, and the owners' personal liability is generally limited. It involves more paperwork and ongoing filing duties than a business name.

There are other structures too, such as partnerships and incorporated trustees, but these two cover most small businesses.

## What registration gets you

Even if no law is forcing you today, registration unlocks things that are hard to get without it:

- **A business bank account.** Many banks want registration documents before opening an account in a business name.
- **Corporate and government clients.** Larger organisations often require a registered supplier with a tax ID before they'll process your invoice.
- **Payment and platform access.** Payment processors and some marketplaces ask for business registration documents.
- **Credibility.** A registration number on your invoice and documents tells a new client you're a real, traceable business.
- **Separation from your personal finances.** With a limited company, business debts and personal debts are generally kept apart, though not in every situation, such as when you sign a personal guarantee.

## When you can reasonably wait

Registration isn't urgent for everyone. You can often wait if:

- You're testing an idea and earning small amounts
- Your customers are individuals who pay you directly
- You don't need a business bank account yet
- Nobody you sell to has asked for registration documents

Waiting is a business decision, not a loophole. If you trade under a name other than your own, the law generally expects that name to be registered, so check your own situation rather than assuming you're covered.

## When registration stops being optional in practice

Here are the signs it's time:

- **A client asks for your registration number** before they'll pay. This is the most common trigger.
- **You're bidding for larger contracts or tenders.** Many require a registered company.
- **You're taking on real financial risk,** such as large orders, leases or staff, where personal liability would hurt.
- **You want investors, partners or grants.** These nearly always need a registered entity.
- **You want to keep your finances separate.** Mixing business and personal money gets messy fast.

## Business name or limited company?

A simple rule of thumb: start with a business name if you're a freelancer or a small local operation, and move to a limited company when contracts get larger, you want liability protection, or bigger clients expect it. Many businesses start with a business name and upgrade later.

If you're unsure, a short conversation with a CAC-accredited agent or an accountant is cheap insurance.

## What it costs and how long it takes

Fees and timelines depend on the structure you choose, your share capital if you're forming a company, and whether you use an agent. Published figures vary widely from site to site, so don't rely on a number from a random blog, including this one. Check the CAC's official portal for current fees, and be cautious about anyone quoting prices that look very different from the official ones.

## Registration is not the same as VAT registration

This trips up a lot of people. Registering your business with the CAC does not automatically mean you charge VAT, and not being VAT-registered doesn't mean you can skip CAC. They're separate questions, handled by different bodies. Whether you need to charge VAT is covered in [do you actually need to register for VAT](/blog/do-you-actually-need-to-register-for-vat), and how to charge it correctly is in [how to calculate VAT in Nigeria](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria).

You'll also want a Tax Identification Number (TIN) on your documents. Confirm your exact obligations with the tax authority or an accountant.

## Put it on your invoice

Once you're registered, use it. Your registration number (RC for companies, BN for business names), your TIN and your full business name belong on every invoice. Corporate clients often can't process payment without them. They're part of the [12-point invoice checklist](/blog/invoice-template-checklist), and they make a real difference to how professional your paperwork looks.

If you're also formalising how you take on work, pair registration with a [simple written agreement before you start each job](/blog/do-you-need-a-contract-before-starting-work).

## The bottom line

You don't have to register on day one. But the moment a client, a bank or a bigger opportunity asks for it, being unregistered costs you more than registering would have. Registering early, even as a simple business name, removes that obstacle before it appears.
