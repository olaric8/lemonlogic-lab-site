A client is three weeks overdue. You've sent the reminders, you've been polite, and now you're thinking: can I charge them extra for this?

It's a fair question, and a common one. The short answer is that late payment fees are generally workable, but mostly when they were agreed in advance and are reasonable. Adding one after the fact, with no warning, is where people run into trouble.

Here's how to think about it. (This is general information, not legal advice, and the details matter if the amount is large.)

## The short answer

- **A late fee or interest term you agreed upfront, in writing, is on solid ground.** Both sides knew the rule before the work started.
- **A fee you invent after the invoice is overdue is hard to enforce.** The client never agreed to it.
- **Interest on an unpaid debt isn't something you can simply add by default.** Legal commentary aimed at Nigerian businesses makes the same point: businesses often assume they can charge interest on unpaid invoices the way a bank does, but without a term in the agreement, claiming it is much harder.

So the real question isn't "are late fees legal?" It's "did the client agree to this before the work began?"

## Why "agreed in advance" matters so much

Most payment arrangements between small businesses are contracts, even when they're informal. What the two sides agreed is the starting point for any dispute. If the agreement says "payment due in 14 days, late payments attract a fee of X," there's something firm to point to. If the agreement says nothing about late payment, you're asking the client to accept a rule they never saw.

That's why a short written agreement matters before you start work. If you haven't read it, here's [what a simple agreement should cover](/blog/do-you-need-a-contract-before-starting-work), including late payment terms.

## Late fee or interest: which one?

Two common approaches:

**A percentage on the overdue amount.** For example, a stated percentage per month on the unpaid balance, starting after the due date. This scales with the size of the invoice.

**A flat fee.** A fixed amount added once an invoice passes its due date. Simpler to explain, but it can feel arbitrary on large invoices.

Either can work. What matters is that it's stated clearly, shown to the client early, and applied consistently.

## What counts as reasonable?

There's no single number that is right for every business, and it can depend on the situation. But as a general principle, a late fee should look like fair compensation for the delay, not a punishment. A charge that looks wildly out of proportion to the actual inconvenience is the kind of term that can be challenged.

Practical guidelines:

- **Keep it modest and proportionate** to the invoice size and the length of the delay.
- **Don't stack charges.** One clear fee beats a pile of overlapping penalties.
- **Make it simple enough to calculate** from the invoice alone.
- **Don't surprise anyone.** If they can't find it in advance, it isn't fair.

For anything large, or for long-term contracts, have a lawyer review the wording.

## Where to put it

A late fee works best when the client sees it more than once:

1. **In your quote or agreement,** before the work starts.
2. **On every invoice,** next to the due date.
3. **In your reminders,** a day or two before the deadline.

Put the due date on the invoice as an actual date, not "net 30". The [invoice checklist](/blog/invoice-template-checklist) covers exactly where payment terms should sit. And if your invoices are missing this, it's one of the most common [invoicing mistakes](/blog/7-invoicing-mistakes-costing-nigerian-businesses).

A simple example of wording (adapt it to your business and have it checked if the stakes are high):

> "Payment is due within 14 days of the invoice date. Invoices unpaid after the due date may be subject to a late fee as agreed in our written terms."

## Should you actually charge it?

Having the right to charge a fee and using it are different things. Many small businesses keep the term in place as a firm incentive and only apply it when a client is persistently late.

Some practical options:

- **Offer a short grace period** before the fee kicks in.
- **Send a reminder first.** Most late payments are forgotten, not deliberate. A polite nudge often solves it. Our guide on [getting clients to pay on time](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time) has wording that works.
- **Waive it for good clients** who slip once, and mention that you did.
- **Consider an early-payment discount instead.** Some clients respond better to a reward than a penalty.

## What not to do

- **Don't add fees retroactively** to invoices that didn't mention them.
- **Don't invent charges** that weren't agreed.
- **Don't threaten legal action you won't take.** It damages your credibility.
- **Don't harass the client.** Keep every message professional and in writing.

## If the client still won't pay

A late fee is a nudge, not a cure. If a client has stopped responding or is refusing outright, follow the steps in [what to do when a client refuses to pay](/blog/what-to-do-when-client-refuses-to-pay). For large amounts, speak to a lawyer before sending any formal demand.
