A client in the UK or US wants to hire you. They're happy to pay, and they ask, "What currency do you invoice in?" For many Nigerian small businesses, that's the first time the question has come up, and the answer affects how much money you actually end up with.

Invoicing a foreign client isn't harder than invoicing a local one, but a few details matter much more. Get them wrong and you can lose a chunk of your payment to fees and exchange rates before it ever reaches you.

## Decide the currency before you start

The most important rule: agree the invoice currency in writing before any work begins.

**Invoice in dollars (or the client's currency)** when you want to protect yourself from the naira losing value between sending the invoice and getting paid. This is common for freelancers, agencies and exporters.

**Invoice in naira** when your costs are mostly in naira and the client is happy to convert. This is simpler for you, but it may be harder for a foreign client to pay.

Whichever you choose, put the currency on the quote, the agreement and the invoice, and name it clearly, for example "USD 1,500" rather than a bare "$1,500". Dollar signs are used by several currencies, so a currency code avoids confusion.

Your pricing should reflect this too. If you're pricing in dollars for the first time, read [how much should you charge](/blog/how-much-should-you-charge) first, and build in the costs of getting paid, which we'll cover below.

## What a foreign-currency invoice should include

Everything from the [12-point invoice checklist](/blog/invoice-template-checklist) still applies. In addition:

1. **The currency, clearly stated** on every amount
2. **The client's full legal name and country,** and their tax or VAT number if they have one
3. **Your payment details for foreign payments.** A local account number alone often isn't enough. International transfers usually need extra details, depending on how you're receiving money
4. **A payment reference** so the client's finance team can match the payment to the invoice
5. **A due date as an actual date,** with the time zone in mind if deadlines are tight
6. **Who pays the transfer fees.** More on this below

If you haven't set up a standard invoice yet, start with [how to write an invoice that gets paid](/blog/how-to-write-an-invoice-that-gets-paid).

## Who pays the fees?

This is the detail that catches people out. International payments often involve fees from the sending bank, sometimes an intermediary bank, and the receiving bank or platform. The amount that lands in your account can be noticeably less than the invoice total.

You have two sensible options:

- **Build the fees into your price,** so you still earn what you need after deductions.
- **State on the invoice** that the amount is to be received in full, with transfer fees covered by the client.

Clients vary in what they accept, so settle this in your agreement, not after the money has come up short. A written [agreement before you start work](/blog/do-you-need-a-contract-before-starting-work) is the right place for it.

## How you get paid: the main routes

There are several ways Nigerian businesses receive foreign payments, and the options, fees and rules change often. In general terms:

- **A domiciliary bank account** lets you hold foreign currency in Nigeria. International wires into it can attract bank and intermediary charges, which can be a big share of small payments but are more reasonable for large ones.
- **Fintech platforms** that give you foreign receiving details (a USD or GBP account number for your client to pay into) are popular with freelancers. Fees, features and availability differ between providers.
- **Freelance marketplaces** pay out through their own systems, with their own fees.
- **Remittance-style services** may pay out in naira rather than dollars, depending on the service and current rules.

Rules from the Central Bank and the availability of specific platforms have changed more than once in recent years, and published comparisons are often written by the platforms themselves. Before you pick a route, check the provider's current terms, compare the total cost on a realistic payment, and confirm any regulatory points with your bank. We're not recommending any particular provider here.

## Protect yourself from exchange-rate swings

If you invoice in naira for a foreign client, you carry the risk that the rate moves between quote and payment. If you invoice in dollars, you carry a different one: the naira value of your payment can change by the time you convert it.

Practical habits:

- **Keep payment terms short.** The longer an invoice sits, the more the rate can move.
- **Ask for a deposit** on larger jobs, so part of your payment arrives early.
- **Use milestone billing** for long projects rather than one big invoice at the end.
- **Record the naira value on the day you receive each payment.** You'll need it for your books.

## Tax and bookkeeping

Income from foreign clients is still business income. Keep proper records of each invoice, the payment date, the amount received, the fees deducted and the exchange rate used. [Simple monthly bookkeeping](/blog/simple-bookkeeping-for-nigerian-smes) makes this manageable.

How VAT applies to services sold to foreign clients can be different from local sales, and the rules have been changing. Don't assume either way. Our guide to [whether you need to register for VAT](/blog/do-you-actually-need-to-register-for-vat) is a good starting point, and for anything involving foreign clients, confirm your treatment with an accountant or the tax authority.

Some foreign clients also prefer to work with a registered business, so it can help to [consider CAC registration](/blog/cac-registration-do-you-need-to-register-your-business).

## When a foreign client pays late

Chasing a client in another country is slower and harder than chasing one in Lagos, which is another reason to settle terms upfront. Write your late payment terms into the agreement, as covered in [what you can actually charge for late payment](/blog/late-payment-fees-nigeria-what-you-can-charge), and send reminders early and in writing. If it comes to a dispute, a [clear quote and signed agreement](/blog/quote-vs-invoice-when-to-send-each) is your best protection.
