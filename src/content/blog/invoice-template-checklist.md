Most invoices that get paid late aren't late because the client is difficult. They're late because the invoice itself was missing something — a bank detail, a due date, a clear description — and the client either couldn't process it or used the gap as an excuse to stall.

An invoice isn't just a bill. It's a document that has to survive someone else's internal approval process, accounting system, and sometimes a slow-moving finance department. The more complete it is, the fewer reasons anyone has to sit on it.

Here's the full checklist — 12 elements every invoice should have, and why each one matters.

## 1. The word "Invoice"

Sounds obvious, but it's the first thing that gets missed when people use a generic Word template. Label the document clearly at the top. If it says "Bill" or nothing at all, some corporate accounting systems won't log it correctly, and it can bounce back to you for clarification — costing you days.

## 2. A unique invoice number

Every invoice needs its own reference number. This isn't optional politeness — it's how both you and your client track payment status, especially once you're issuing more than a handful a month.

A simple sequential system works fine: INV-001, INV-002, and so on. If you invoice multiple businesses, you can prefix by client or year (INV-2026-001) to keep things organized as volume grows.

Without a number, if a client says "we already paid that one," you have no clean way to confirm which invoice they mean.

## 3. Your business details

Full business name, address, phone number, and email. If you're VAT registered, your VAT number needs to be here too — [this is a legal requirement once you're registered](/blog/do-you-actually-need-to-register-for-vat), not optional formatting.

If you operate as a registered company, include your RC number. Corporate clients often need this for their own compliance records, and not having it can delay processing on their end.

## 4. The client's details

Full name of the business (not just a contact person's name), their address, and the specific contact who requested the work. If you're invoicing a company but a specific department handles payments, note that too.

This matters more than it seems — invoices addressed vaguely ("to whom it may concern") are far more likely to get lost in a company's internal routing before they reach anyone who can approve payment.

## 5. Invoice date

The date you're issuing the invoice. This is different from the due date, and different from the date you delivered the work. Clients use this to calculate payment terms, so it needs to be accurate and clearly labeled.

## 6. Payment due date

Not "payment terms: 30 days" — an actual date. "Net 30" requires the client to do math and assumes they know exactly when they received the invoice. A specific date ("Due: 15 October 2026") removes any ambiguity and gives you a firm point to reference when following up.

This single change is one of the most effective things you can do to [get paid faster without sounding pushy](/blog/how-to-get-clients-to-pay-on-time) — it turns a vague expectation into a concrete deadline both sides can point to.

## 7. Clear description of goods or services

Vague line items ("Consulting - ₦150,000") invite questions and delay approval, especially in companies where someone other than your contact has to sign off on the invoice before payment is released.

Be specific: "Website homepage redesign — 3 revision rounds, delivered 12 September 2026." The more self-explanatory the invoice is, the less back-and-forth is needed before it gets approved internally.

## 8. Quantity, rate, and line totals

If you're billing for multiple items or hours, break it down:

| Item | Qty | Rate | Total |
|------|-----|------|-------|
| Logo design | 1 | ₦45,000 | ₦45,000 |
| Website design | 1 | ₦150,000 | ₦150,000 |

This isn't just for clarity — it's often required for the client's own internal accounting, especially if they need to allocate costs across different budget lines.

## 9. Subtotal, VAT, and total

Show the subtotal before tax, the VAT amount separately, and the final total. [VAT in Nigeria is 7.5%](/blog/how-to-calculate-vat-nigeria), and it needs to be itemized — not folded silently into your prices. Clients who are VAT registered themselves need this breakdown to reclaim input VAT on their own returns, and an invoice without it can get rejected by their finance team outright.

## 10. Payment details

Bank name, account number, and account name — exactly as it appears at the bank. Mismatched names between the invoice and the bank account are one of the most common reasons a transfer gets delayed while someone double-checks they're sending money to the right place.

If you accept multiple payment methods (bank transfer, POS, mobile money), list them all here rather than making the client ask.

## 11. Notes or terms

A short line for anything the client needs to know — late payment terms, whether a deposit was already received and deducted, or a thank-you note. This is also where you'd mention any discount applied and why, so nothing looks like a pricing error later.

## 12. A way to reach you

A phone number or email specifically for payment questions, even if it's the same one already on the invoice header. Clients paying late are sometimes stuck, not stalling — they might be waiting on an internal approval and need to ask you something. Make it easy for them to reach you instead of just letting the invoice sit unanswered.

## What happens when one of these is missing

In our experience running invoicing across several businesses, the most common gaps aren't dramatic — they're small omissions that create just enough friction to delay payment:

- No due date → client assumes "whenever is fine" and deprioritizes it
- No VAT breakdown → gets bounced back by their accounts team
- Mismatched account name → payment held while they verify
- Vague description → routed to someone else for clarification before approval

None of these are the client being difficult. They're just doing their job carefully, and an incomplete invoice gives them a reason to pause rather than pay.
